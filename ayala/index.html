Volunteering in the Philippines: What a First Day Is Like

The short answerBetween 23% and 44% of Filipinos volunteered in 2024, depending on which survey you read. Willingness is not the constraint. For most first-time volunteers the obstacle is simply not knowing what the day involves: when to arrive, what to wear, what the task is, and when it ends.

Key takeaways

  • Two credible surveys put Filipino volunteering in 2024 at 23% (Charities Aid Foundation) and 44% (Gallup) - the methods differ, so the true figure is unsettled.
  • The Philippine Statistics Authority began running a Volunteer Work Survey in May 2025 precisely because reliable volunteering data did not exist.
  • Republic Act 9418, the Volunteer Act of 2007, makes promoting volunteerism state policy.
  • Filipinos prefer helping directly: 54% would rather give to a person or family than to an organisation (Charities Aid Foundation, 2025).
  • A first volunteer day is four hours or so: sign in, a briefing huddle, a task with an end, and people eating together afterwards.

How many Filipinos actually volunteer?

Two respected surveys tried to count how many Filipinos volunteered in 2024, and they came back with very different answers.

The Charities Aid Foundation's World Giving Report 2025, produced with the Association of Foundations, put Filipino volunteering at 23%. Gallup's data, released in February 2025, put it at 44% - enough to rank the Philippines fourth in the world for volunteering, behind only Indonesia, Liberia and Kenya, and down from 48% the year before.

Both figures come from careful work. The two surveys disagree because they ask different questions of different samples over different recall periods. The gap itself is the more useful finding: volunteering in the Philippines is real and large, but it is loosely measured, largely informal, and mostly invisible in the official record.

The Philippine government reached the same conclusion. In May 2025 the Philippine Statistics Authority began running a Volunteer Work Survey as a rider on the Labour Force Survey, commissioned by the Philippine National Volunteer Service Coordinating Agency (PNVSCA), explicitly to “produce reliable data on volunteerism within the labour force” and to show “the potential economic contributions of volunteer work.”

Is willingness the problem?

No. The instinct is already there, and in the Philippines it has a name. Bayanihan is the Filipino practice of neighbours turning up to help one another without being asked or paid - most literally, a whole community carrying a house to a new site.

It is also written into Philippine law. Republic Act 9418, the Volunteer Act of 2007, approved on 10 April 2007, makes it state policy to promote the participation of Filipino society in volunteer work, and mandates PNVSCA to coordinate volunteer programmes as part of national development.

What the research suggests is that this willingness travels through people rather than institutions. The Charities Aid Foundation found that Filipinos strongly prefer helping directly: 42.3% of donations went straight to people in need against 27.7% to charities, and when asked how they would rather give, 54% chose a person or family over 31% who chose an organisation.

Scale matters for reading those percentages. The Philippine Statistics Authority recorded 49.21 million employed Filipinos in July 2026, so even the lower 23% volunteering estimate describes millions of people giving time each year.

Writing in the University of the Philippines' Public Policy journal in 2002, Ramon L. Fernan III observed the same pattern in Filipino charity - that it “appears to be primarily directed at family members and friends, and, to a certain extent, towards the immediate community,” and that “formal fundraising is often facilitated by personal networks that take this penchant for a personal, or at least familiar, connection into account.”

The Fernan paper does not measure how volunteers are recruited, and it should not be read as if it did. But the direction is consistent across two decades and several instruments: Filipinos help where they already know somebody.

Why is the first day the real obstacle?

If help moves through relationships, then the hardest volunteer day is the one where the volunteer has no relationships yet.

Consider what a first-timer is actually being asked to do: arrive somewhere unfamiliar, alone; not know where to stand; not know whether they are dressed correctly, whether they will be useful, or whether they will be in the way of people who clearly know what they are doing; and not know how long it lasts.

None of that is about generosity. It is social risk, and it is almost never addressed in the material that asks people to sign up. Volunteer recruitment copy tends to describe the cause, sometimes the need, occasionally the impact. It rarely describes the morning.

What does a first volunteer day actually look like?

A first volunteer day is smaller and more ordinary than the invitation makes it sound. It usually runs like this:

  1. You sign in at a folding table and get a name tag.
  2. You stand in a briefing huddle where somebody - often a resident who knows the site - explains what is happening and what you will be doing.
  3. You learn three or four names.
  4. You work, usually beside someone who has done it before and who will tell you, without ceremony, if you are holding the tool wrong.
  5. It ends, and people eat together, and that part is not on the schedule.

The shape of it is visible in real listings. On 24 October 2026 a tree-planting activity runs at La Mesa Ecopark, marking BPI's 175th anniversary. An International Coastal Cleanup at Baseco Beach in Manila ran from 4:00 to 9:00 AM, and the brief told volunteers exactly what to bring: comfortable clothes and boots, a hat or rain gear, sunblock, and a reusable water bottle. Some Ayala Foundation activities are open to anyone; others are reserved for Ayala group employees, and each listing says which.

That level of specificity is the whole point. A cause explains why somebody should help. A schedule, a task with an end, and a list of what to wear explain whether they can actually come.

What this means for anyone thinking about it

The Philippines does not have a volunteering shortage. Depending on which survey you believe, somewhere between a fifth and nearly half of the adult population volunteered last year, and the state has legislated to encourage more.

What the country has is a description problem. The gap between “I would like to help” and “I turned up on a Saturday” is narrow, and it is crossed by knowing what the day looks like - ideally from someone who has already done it, which is how Filipinos have always been recruited into anything.

Livelihood Programmes Count Trainees, Not Income

The short answerThe Philippine Sustainable Livelihood Program reached 156.6% of its household target in 2022, yet independent evaluation found no significant effect on beneficiary income, expenditure or savings. Reach was exceeded; income was not moved. The gap is a measurement problem: programmes count people trained, not money earned.

Key takeaways

  • DSWD's Sustainable Livelihood Program served 240,668 households against a 153,684 target - 156.60% of plan (Commission on Audit, CY2022).
  • PIDS found the same programme had no significant effect on income, expenditure, savings or capital expenditure, with a benefit-cost ratio substantially below 1.
  • 62.8% of SLP microenterprises were still operating; among closed individual projects, 61.8% cited failure to earn money as the reason.
  • Skills supply is not the visible failure: 83.34% of TESDA TVET graduates were employed, 98.25% within a year - but median earnings were PHP 11,000 a month and 36.14% were own-account workers.
  • MSMEs hold just 4.6% of outstanding loans against about 21% in benchmark countries (DTI MSMED Plan 2023-2028).

What do Philippine livelihood programmes actually deliver?

In calendar year 2022 the Department of Social Welfare and Development's Sustainable Livelihood Program served 240,668 households against a target of 153,684 - 156.60% of plan, according to the Commission on Audit's annual audit report on DSWD.

By the standard the programme reports against, that is a considerable success. Reach was not just met but exceeded by more than half again.

Now set it beside the outcome. The Philippine Institute for Development Studies, evaluating the same programme, found no significant effect on beneficiary income, expenditure, savings or capital expenditure, and a benefit-cost ratio substantially less than unity. The programme reached far more people than it promised, and the money in their pockets did not measurably change.

Both findings are true at once. That is the uncomfortable part, and it is not explained by a lack of effort, funding or scale.

Is the problem that people lack skills?

The evidence says no, at least not at the headline level. 83.34% of Philippine TVET graduates were employed according to TESDA's Study on Employment of TVET Graduates - the highest rate since the pandemic, up 5.49 percentage points - with 98.25% placed within a year and an average of 4.31 months to placement. The assessment pass rate was 97.71%.

Those are not the numbers of a system failing to train people or failing to place them. Any argument that Filipinos are trained and then left with nowhere to go does not survive contact with TESDA's own data, and should not be made.

The strain shows up in job quality instead. Median monthly earnings for TVET graduates were PHP 11,000 (mean PHP 14,129.49), and 36.14% were own-account workers - people whose income depends not on a payslip but on finding buyers, week by week. Nationally, the Philippine Statistics Authority recorded an underemployment rate of 12.9% in July 2026, about one in eight employed Filipinos, of whom 56.7% were visibly underemployed at under 40 hours a week. Underemployment has been falling - it was 14.8% a year earlier - so the honest reading is the level, not the direction.

Why do community enterprises close?

PIDS found 62.8% of SLP microenterprises still operating - 71.7% of group projects against 49.1% of individual ones. So roughly half of individual enterprises had stopped.

The reason participants themselves gave is the most direct evidence available. Among closed individual projects, 61.8% cited failure to earn money. Among closed group projects, 29.8% cited not making money and 27.7% cited a lack of member participation.

This is self-reported rather than causally identified, and it deserves to be read as such. PIDS did not run a test isolating market access as the binding constraint, and for group enterprises the second-largest reason - members not turning up - is an organisational problem, not a demand one.

Is market access the missing piece?

Partly, and less cleanly than the sector usually claims. Three verified findings point the same way.

First, credit. MSMEs account for 1,241,733 establishments, or 99.63% of all registered establishments in the Philippines, and employ about two-thirds of the people working in registered establishments. Yet they hold only 4.6% of total outstanding loans, against roughly 21% in benchmark countries, according to the Department of Trade and Industry's MSME Development Plan 2023-2028.

Second, the terms of sale. In PIDS's endline evaluation of the ConVERGE project, marketing agreements across all agrarian reform community clusters were informal and verbal, and disadvantageous to producer organisations - in one case a buyer simply refused lower-grade output.

Third, and against interest: market-linkage programming has not demonstrated income gains either. In that same ConVERGE endline, beneficiary household incomes fell 26%, eight percentage points less than the decline among non-beneficiaries. A cushioned fall during a period of pandemic and price shocks is a real result, but it is not growth, and anyone promising that market linkage delivers what training did not is overstating the evidence.

So what should be counted instead?

If reach can be exceeded by 57% while income stays flat, then reach is the wrong headline metric. The measures that would have caught the gap are the ones an enterprise already tracks for itself:

  1. Revenue actually received by the enterprise, by month, not the value of assets granted to it.
  2. Whether a buyer exists, is named, and is under written rather than verbal agreement.
  3. Repeat orders, which is the only real evidence a market relationship survived the programme.
  4. Working capital available after the grant is spent, given MSMEs hold 4.6% of outstanding loans.
  5. Survival at 24 months, disaggregated between group and individual enterprises, which behave very differently (71.7% against 49.1%).

None of that is exotic. It is what a community enterprise counts anyway: not the people it trained, but the crates it shipped.

Filipino Students Don't Lack Ambition. They Lack Directions

The short answer51% of Filipino students expect to be professionals by age 30 and 85% expect to reach college, against a labour market where 6% of workers aged 30 to 40 are professionals. Only 2.5% named a guidance counsellor as a source of advice on choosing a course. The shortage is not ambition - it is directions.

Key takeaways

  • Filipino students aspire high: 51% expect to be professionals by 30 and 85% expect to reach college (ADB with DepEd), against 6% of workers aged 30-40 who are professionals.
  • Only 2.5% of students named a guidance counsellor as a source of advice on their college course - the least-cited source. Parents were named by 68.8% and peers by 48.3%.
  • DepEd reported roughly 5,000 registered guidance counsellors for more than 45,000 schools, and fewer than 300 people graduated with an MA in Guidance Counseling nationwide in SY 2023-2024.
  • Filipino women are 53.4% of researchers by headcount (UNESCO Institute for Statistics, 2020) - so 'girls cannot see themselves in science' is the wrong framing for this country.
  • In an Asian Development Bank randomised trial in Bhutan, mentoring raised first-preference for a science stream by 16.2 percentage points but had no statistically significant effect on actual enrolment.

Do Filipino students lack ambition?

The opposite. In a survey of 3,172 students across 238 schools conducted for the Asian Development Bank with the Department of Education, 51% of Filipino students said they wanted to be professionals by the age of 30, 72.6% of their parents expected the same, and 85% expected to go to college.

Set that against the labour market the same report cites: in the 2016 Labour Force Survey, 6% of Filipino workers aged 30 to 40 were professionals, and 24% held elementary occupations.

The measurable problem in the Philippines is therefore an aspiration surplus relative to opportunity, not an aspiration deficit. Any programme designed to make Filipino young people want more is solving a problem the data does not show.

Are Filipino girls underrepresented in science?

Not as researchers, and this matters because it is the assumption most often made. According to the UNESCO Institute for Statistics, 53.4% of Philippine researchers were women in 2020 by headcount, and 52.4% on a full-time-equivalent basis. Filipino women are a majority of the country's research workforce.

The Philippine gender gap in schooling also runs the other way. In PISA 2022, Filipino girls outperformed boys by 14 points in mathematics and 35 points in reading, and the Philippines was one of only 17 participating countries where girls beat boys in mathematics. Women were 55.6% of higher education enrolment in AY 2017-18, and female functional literacy was 74.1% against 67.6% for males in the 2024 FLEMMS.

There is a narrower, real deficit worth naming precisely. The female share of Philippine tertiary STEM graduates was 38.3% in 2024 and has been flat to falling for a decade, and the share of female graduates in ICT fields fell from 10.1% in 2015 to 5.4% in 2024. That is a field-level and field-of-study problem, not an evidence base for the claim that Filipino girls cannot picture themselves in science.

Where do Filipino students actually get career advice?

From people they already know, and almost never from a professional. In the same ADB and DepEd survey, students named the following as sources of advice on choosing a college course:

Source of adviceShare of students naming it
Parents68.8%
Peers48.3%
Relatives40.5%
Guidance counsellors2.5%

Guidance counsellors were the least-cited source. The structural reason is a workforce shortage. A DepEd undersecretary said publicly in August 2026 that the country has “only ... a thousand registered guidance counselors ... for more than 45,000 schools,” with roughly 5,000 registered nationwide as of 31 January. The Second Congressional Commission on Education reported in January 2026 that fewer than 300 people graduated with a master's in Guidance Counseling nationwide in SY 2023-2024.

So the advice that determines a Filipino student's course, campus and career comes overwhelmingly from parents and friends - people who care, but who may never have navigated the specific path the student is considering.

Does exposure to a role model change what students do?

It changes what they say they want, and the evidence that it changes what they do is weak. This is the most important caveat in this article.

The Asian Development Bank ran a randomised controlled trial of locally-recruited mentors with grade 10 and grade 12 students - the closest rigorous test of the mechanism available in Asia. Mentoring raised the share of grade 10 students naming a science stream as their first preference by 16.2 percentage points against a control mean of 43.6%, significant at 1%. It had no statistically significant effect on whether those students actually switched to a STEM-related stream the following academic year. For grade 12 students, mentoring raised consideration of TVET by 5.9 points and moved application, admission and enrolment by nothing measurable at nine months.

That trial was conducted in Bhutan, not the Philippines, and should not be presented as Philippine evidence. No Philippine impact evaluation of mentoring, near-peer role models or career guidance on enrolment or persistence appears to exist. A separate randomised study in the United Kingdom found role-model effects on aspiration and effort that “dissipated within a few weeks.”

There is also direct evidence about what Filipino students themselves say drives their choices. DOST-SEI's Appreciating Science study of 806 senior high school students ranked their reasons in this order: economic reasons and financial support first, teacher competency and teaching style second, school environment and laboratory facilities third, innate interest fourth. Role models do not appear on the list. It is a purposive sample rather than a nationally representative one, but it is the students' own account.

What follows from this

Taken together, the Philippine evidence points somewhere more specific than inspiration. Students already want the outcome. Women are already a majority of researchers. What is thin is the supply of people who can explain the route - which course, which requirements, what it costs, how long before you earn - and who can be asked a second question a month later.

On that reading, the useful contribution of someone a few years ahead is not that they are inspiring. It is that they are navigable: they know the steps, they are from the same street, and they can be asked again. A single career talk is unlikely to do it, given how quickly measured effects decay.

Frequently asked questions

How many guidance counsellors are there in the Philippines?

A DepEd undersecretary said in August 2026 that there are around a thousand registered guidance counsellors for more than 45,000 schools, with roughly 5,000 registered nationwide as of 31 January. Fewer than 300 people graduated with a master's in Guidance Counseling in SY 2023-2024, according to EDCOM II.

Do Filipino students want to go to college?

Yes, overwhelmingly. In a survey for the Asian Development Bank with DepEd, 85% of Filipino students expected to go to college and 51% expected to be professionals by age 30. In the labour market they were entering, 6% of workers aged 30 to 40 were professionals.

Are women underrepresented in science in the Philippines?

Not among researchers. UNESCO Institute for Statistics data shows 53.4% of Philippine researchers were women in 2020 by headcount. The narrower gap is in fields of study: women were 38.3% of tertiary STEM graduates in 2024, and the female share of ICT graduates has fallen since 2015.

Who advises Filipino students on choosing a course?

Mostly family and friends. In the ADB and DepEd survey, 68.8% of students named parents, 48.3% named peers and 40.5% named relatives. Only 2.5% named a guidance counsellor, making counsellors the least-cited source of advice.

Does mentoring increase STEM enrolment?

The strongest evidence available in Asia says it raises stated preference but not enrolment. An Asian Development Bank randomised trial in Bhutan raised first-preference for a science stream by 16.2 percentage points but found no statistically significant effect on students actually switching streams. No equivalent Philippine trial appears to exist.

How did the Philippines perform in PISA?

In PISA 2025, 21% of Filipino students reached Level 2 or above in mathematics, 31% in reading and 32% in science, against OECD averages of 65%, 69% and 74%. Results improved on both 2022 and 2018, with low performers down 11 percentage points in reading since 2018.
